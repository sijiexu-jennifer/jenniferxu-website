Why One Year of Income Can Mislead

Matching parents and children with SQL in Python

INTERGENERATIONAL MOBILITY

How much of the income gap between parents carries into the next generation? The intergenerational elasticity of income (IGE) summarizes that persistence. An IGE of 0.40 means that a 10% difference in parental income is associated with about a 4% difference in child income. Estimating the regression is easy; constructing the right parent-child sample and measuring long-run income are the difficult parts.

This tutorial uses SQL inside Python to do both. The data are simulated so the complete workflow is public and reproducible. They contain 1,200 families, stable person and family identifiers, and repeated annual incomes. The numerical estimates illustrate a measurement problem; they are not evidence about a real population.

Step 1: Match parents and children

The people table has one row per person, with person_id, family_id, and role. A self-join turns those individual records into parent-child pairs.

SELECT
    c.person_id AS child_id,
    p.person_id AS parent_id,
    c.family_id
FROM people AS c
JOIN people AS p
  ON c.family_id = p.family_id
 AND c.role = 'child'
 AND p.role = 'parent';

SELECT chooses the columns returned by the query. JOIN combines rows, and the ON clause defines a valid relationship: the two people must share a family identifier while holding different roles. Requiring both conditions avoids child-child and parent-parent matches. In real administrative or survey data, I would also check whether family identifiers are stable over time and how step-parents, adoptive parents, and multiple guardians are coded.

Step 2: Aggregate annual income

The matched IDs are joined to the income table. The query uses separate common table expressions for parents and children so that repeated observations do not create a many-to-many row explosion.

WITH parent_measures AS (
  SELECT m.family_id, m.parent_id,
    MAX(CASE WHEN i.year = 2000 THEN i.income END)
      AS parent_income_single,
    AVG(CASE WHEN i.year BETWEEN 1998 AND 2002 THEN i.income END)
      AS parent_income_multi
  FROM matches AS m
  JOIN income AS i ON m.parent_id = i.person_id
  GROUP BY m.family_id, m.parent_id
),
child_measures AS (
  SELECT m.family_id, m.child_id,
    MAX(CASE WHEN i.year = 2000 THEN i.income END)
      AS child_income_single,
    AVG(CASE WHEN i.year BETWEEN 2006 AND 2010 THEN i.income END)
      AS child_income_multi
  FROM matches AS m
  JOIN income AS i ON m.child_id = i.person_id
  GROUP BY m.family_id, m.child_id
)
SELECT p.*, c.child_id, c.child_income_single, c.child_income_multi
FROM parent_measures AS p
JOIN child_measures AS c ON p.family_id = c.family_id;

GROUP BY creates one row per person-family combination. MAX(CASE WHEN ...) extracts the single qualifying year, while AVG(CASE WHEN ...) averages the selected years. Parents are measured at ages 38-42 and children at ages 31-35. The generations need not be observed in the same calendar years; comparable career stages matter more.

Step 3: Estimate the IGE

Python estimates the log-log regression

\[\log(Y_{child})=\alpha+\beta\log(Y_{parent})+\varepsilon,\]

where \(\beta\) is the IGE. Heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors describe sampling uncertainty.

Income definition Child age(s) IGE Robust SE 95% CI
One year 25 0.155 0.015 [0.125, 0.185]
Five-year average 31-35 0.386 0.022 [0.343, 0.429]

The five-year estimate is close to the simulation’s long-run persistence parameter of 0.40. The age-25 estimate is less than half as large. This is not evidence that mobility suddenly changed; it is a warning that the income measure changed.

Why the child’s age matters

Income at age 25 can be a poor proxy for permanent economic status. People with high eventual earnings may still be in graduate school, training, or low-paid entry positions. Annual earnings also contain temporary bonuses, layoffs, illness, and measurement error. When those early-career or transitory components weaken the observed parent-child relationship, the estimated IGE is biased downward.

The age profile makes that mechanism visible. Holding the parental five-year average fixed, the single-year IGE is only 0.183 at age 25 and returns to roughly 0.40 at later ages. Averaging child income across ages 31-35 then removes additional year-to-year noise.

Takeaway

SQL makes the construction of an intergenerational sample auditable: identifiers define relationships, JOIN combines records, and grouped aggregation converts annual observations into economically meaningful measures. But a technically correct match does not guarantee a credible mobility estimate. Before interpreting an IGE, ask whose income was measured, at what age, and over how many years. In this exercise, replacing a single early-career observation with multi-year averages changes the answer from 0.155 to 0.386.

Methods and data note

code/analysis.py generates the simulated people and income tables with a fixed random seed, loads them into an in-memory SQLite database, runs the SQL queries, estimates ordinary least squares models with HC1 robust standard errors, and saves every table and figure. The README provides exact replication instructions. Because the data-generating process is designed to demonstrate life-cycle bias, the results should be read as a tutorial rather than an empirical claim about U.S. mobility.
